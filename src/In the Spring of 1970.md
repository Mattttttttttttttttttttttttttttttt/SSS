# In the Spring of 1970 | March 22 2026  
A dim strand of moonlight cast a fuzzy shadow of the man on the ground. “Remain calm. You are safe,” he repeated. “In your own words, please tell me what happened.”  
I opened my mouth, but no sound came out. I swallowed instead.  
“Try to start from the beginning.”  
And so I did.

Chapelsville is not quite a metropolis and not quite a small town, you know. People know each other—I like that part about Chapelsville. I’ve known Charles and Lucy for maybe a decade now. Met in high school, Charles and I went on to college, and Lucy pursued her actress career. Oh, she had a real talent\! You should’ve seen her on stage. She wept and rejoiced with her character—there’s nothing like it.  
Charles is one of a kind too. I think you would know that by now. He always has been the “genius” at my high school, but people always took offense at his condescending nature. He means well, though. Under his offputting appearance is a kind heart. When we went to college, all three of us—Charles, Lucy, and me—shared an apartment. During the day, Lucy worked various jobs and auditioned for roles. Charles studied a field of mathematics, and I believe he was really accomplished. Meanwhile, I was just wasting time in school, working some jobs, having fun while I’m still young, you know. Those were the good times, but then something changed.  
I think it all started with when I moved out of that apartment. That was a couple years ago. I landed an office job and moved out. I suppose that’s what that was: we sort of fell out of touch for a while. My job required me to keep late hours—and it still does; Lucy was still struggling to get by, but she was doing better; Charles was presumably writing some research papers. When we all got together again, there was something in the air that I couldn’t quite grasp. Perhaps we lost the naiveness we had. Perhaps we’ve all changed while we were away. It made me miss my high school days.  
But anyways, I digress. Lucy always had to travel to the neighboring cities to perform, because Chapelsville didn’t have a theater. But on Wednesday, Charles called me. Apparently I had missed a dozen calls from him already—he had been trying to get a hold of me all week. Then he told me that a *brand new* theater is opening in Chapelsville, here, and that Lucy landed the role of the main character in the opening show. Could you imagine that\! The best talent playing the starring role\! Charles told me that the final rehearsal is happening today. Naturally I cannot miss this, so I immediately agreed to attend. I had been to very few of Lucy’s acts since I moved out, and I thought that we could use this as an opportunity to catch up, you know.  
It was a nice, comfortable day today—who could’ve seen something as horrible as this coming\! It’s one of those days where the sun is out just enough to make you feel warm. I was walking down Main Street when the flowers in front of this small corner shop caught my attention. I think it was called Jones’s Goodies or something along those lines. Suddenly it occurred to me that I could buy a little something for Lucy, so I went into the store and wandered around. It was a very quaint store, I remember, with a nice smell of wood in the air. It felt like somewhere Charles would spend his entire afternoon at. There were bookshelves that reached the ceilings, and it reminded me of the good days again. I thought the day was off to a good start.  
So I was walking around, and I decided to buy her perfume. She had always loved flowery perfumes. I went up to the perfume section, and picked out one tiny bottle of just that, standing on the counter. Then this very delicate, golden keychain caught my eye. It was a woman gracefully dancing. I thought it was nice, but next to it, I saw a real postcard of Lucy—it was the last act I went to see, and although it was real popular, I hadn’t known she made such a fame of her own\! Of course I took the postcard, and there was a stack of letter paper next to it with envelopes, so I bought all three, and wrote a short note: “Your last act was brilliant. Congratulations on landing the leading role\!”  
It was then I got the feeling that I had been writing these exact words in this exact place before. I looked around me. There was no one, except for another young man of my age, and an old man, who’s probably the owner of the shop. I remember seeing them here too, but I have *never* been to this part of the city, let alone this corner store. Suddenly the scent of wood in the air smelled weird, and the walls seemed too stuffed with things. It was a weird feeling, so I quickly paid for my gifts and hurried out of the store.  
The sun had hidden behind the clouds when I arrived at the theater. Taking a deep breath, I reminded myself of how it used to be. I would get to the theater early, chat with Lucy and Charles backstage while other actors and actresses came in. I reminded myself that everything will be fine; we will chat like we used to, and it’ll be like no time has passed.  
I found Lucy backstage as usual. It struck me that I haven’t seen her in at least a few months. She was a very habitual person, but it was obvious that her living standards had improved. She had a new road trunk, and her outfit looked fashionable. She used to smoke a kind of peculiar, yellow cigarette—even that changed. She was smoking one of those cigarettes that the rich people smoked. I would be lying if I said that the change didn’t throw me off.  
But Lucy smiled, and I smiled back. The ice melted.  
“So good to see you again\!” she chirped.  
“Same, haven’t seen you in so long,” I whispered warmly, and pulled her in for a hug. “You changed so much I barely recognized you\! Case in point: you got rid of those stupid yellow cigarettes—smoking rich people’s cigarettes now. Now how many times have I told you not to smoke so much though; you will ruin your health before you know it.”  
“‘Rich people’s cigarettes’?” she laughed. “Truly, it tastes different too\! It almost feels like I’m inhaling wealth. You, you changed too\! Since you got that office job, you look almost classy.”  
“I’m deeply insulted.” I pretended to be angry, which made her laugh again. “But I’m a really kind person, so I bought perfume for you\!” I searched my bag and held out the small bottle from my bag excitedly. “And also—”  
“Walter, are you reading my mind?” Lucy cried, and hugged me again. “I was looking for one of these for so long\! I can’t thank you enough for this.”  
“Really\! I saw these at a corner store. . . I could show you when you are free—”  
The door opened behind me, and Charles came in. He was frowning, but relaxed when he saw me. “Hey Walter\! Didn’t see you there.” he said through a faint smile. “You’ve changed so much that I didn’t recognize you.”  
“That’s what I said to her\!” I exclaimed, right at the same time as Lucy said the same thing about me, and I started laughing at the absurdity of the situation. It suddenly dawned on me that we hadn’t really had a moment like this since I’ve moved out. It really came into perspective that we had all grown, and it was not 3 teenagers laughing together anymore.  
Behind Charles, a tall man pushed open the half-closed door, and let himself in. It was obvious from the first glance that he’s an athlete. I wouldn’t be surprised if he plays on a professional football team. Lucy’s face seemed to shine when she saw him, and she rushed up to him and hugged him tightly. He patted her back awkwardly, but not meeting our eyes. The motion looked almost mechanical. Who is he, what is he doing here, and why does Lucy know him? Confused, I looked at Charles, but he had already raised his guard back up, and was now analyzing the stranger with a hard gaze.  
Lucy pulled apart from him, and grinned, “Sorry about that, Walter. This is Gary, my boyfriend.” Her face was flushed with happiness, and it made me smile too.  
“I’m so happy for you\!” I said cheerfully. A quick glance to Charles revealed that he’s still evaluating the stranger. Perhaps that’s why he made few friends. Poor Charles, if it weren’t for his judgmental nature, I’m sure that he would be really popular.  
I reached out to Gary for a handshake, and said firmly, “Pleased to meet you, Gary.”  
Gary returned a smile, “You too.”  
The door opened again, and this time it was Mary and Anne, with Julia trailing behind them. They are just a few of the actresses I know from having Lucy as my friend. I’ve also known them for long enough that I don’t remember where we met.  
For Mary, however, I remember. We met at a nearby town’s New Year’s Eve performance. Mary made a really distinct impression on me, because Charles and I found her crying in a dark corner when we got backstage. It turned out she was rather devastated, because Lucy, not her, was chosen as the main character. Being one to turn away from conflicts, I had never been in a situation like this. But Charles had had similar experiences, and could sympathize with her, so we all became friends. Since then, Mary and Lucy had more or less been neck-to-neck competitors in this profession, but recently, Lucy had clearly acquired the fame Mary was after.  
But Mary seemed happy today. And I was glad she got over her insistence to be the best.  
As for Anne, I don’t quite know anything about her, other than the fact that she’s head over heels for Charles. It’s really quite obvious for anyone who pays attention to social interactions, and both Lucy and I figured it out very quickly. Charles, however, never saw what was so obvious to us. I must say poor Charles again—I often lament all the things he’s missing out on, simply *because* of his genius. You see, I think his genius made him *too* rational and idealist, and it really distances people from him. His genius has never let him down, and. . . although he is totally wrong, he meant well.  
I digress again. Julia is someone quite quiet, much quieter than both Mary and Anne, but she has a distinct talent for acting too. On stage, she is a whole different person than her offstage self. Lucy introduced us at some point, and Julia seemed to be good friends with her. I think I remember Lucy telling me that Julia was really supportive of her acting career, and always lended her a hand when she was struggling financially. That was back when she was still really young, struggling to make ends meet, and hadn’t quite had as much restraint in her financial choices. Julia never argued with Lucy about it though, and she never looked mad. I suppose she is like me, always looking to stay out of trouble. But I never got to really know Julia. Maybe she keeps her personality to herself too much.  
I shook hands heartily with all of the actresses, and excused myself out of the actors’ area—the “green room”—because I wanted to walk around the theater. Through the wooden door was an eerily quiet hallway, save the chatter of the actresses behind the closed door. There was a golden pattern imprinted on the ceiling, almost like a dragon. My childhood theaters never had those. To this day, I still remember everything about the evening when I first went to a theater, despite how stripped-down it was.  
I followed the dragon down the hallway, but I actually went down the wrong way accidentally, and arrived at a dead end. There were two steel emergency exit doors at the end, with the “EXIT” box glowing red on top of them. Onto one of the steel doors, however, was taped a piece of paper. “Valiant effort at an exit, but you cannot escape from someone among you,” I read under my breath, and chills ran down my spine as I frowned.  
It suddenly came to my attention that the noise at the end of the hallway had grown louder, which means the wooden door I had passed through earlier was open. So naturally, like any sane person, I hurriedly concealed myself behind a corner in fear of being discovered here by someone. I needed some time to figure out what this all means: first the spooky experience at that corner store, and now this. Bad signs.  
The noise level fell quiet again, and I peeked around the corner. There was no one in the hallway, so I rushed through the hallway as fast as I could, and only stopped once I was in the audience area.  
It occurred to me that I was so preoccupied by my nerves of reunioning with Lucy that I barely paid any attention to the theater itself. It is furnished with the utmost grandeur, with five brand new spotlights hanging from the ceiling. On the ceiling was the dragon pattern again, repeated in tandem. The seats are all of the new flip-up kind, and they were folded up. There was no one else in the enormous auditorium, and the uncanniness came again. I shook it off, and wandered around in the auditorium before Gary emerged from the hallway. With someone in the same space as me, I felt much more comfortable, enough to even strike up a conversation with Gary. But I soon found out that Charles had his reasons to be reserved about Gary. It was clear to me that there was something quite off about him. He appeared like a kind person, but it was plain that there was something on his mind. And in hindsight, I suppose I did notice that he was glancing in the direction of the green room quite often.  
Charles joined us after several minutes. “They are almost done preparing. The show will start soon,” he said matter-of-factly as he sat down next to me.  
Oh, and what a beautiful show it was\! I grew more and more convinced that I will not see anything as admirable as it again, my soul being wholly absorbed into the play. So it was my natural tendency to stand up to applaud the genius of Lucy, as she finished her end-of-play monologue, and collapsed on stage. The clap sounded crisp and resonated around the entire theater—this could be one of the biggest theaters I’ve ever been to.  
But on stage, everyone froze. Suddenly it was evident to me that her collapsing was not in the script. A sense of panic set in. I jumped on stage and gathered around her with everyone else. Gary tested her pulse with his hands, and declared solemnly, “No pulse.”  
There was a second too long of complete silence, before I burst into babbling, “Has she fainted? Why is there no pulse? When I saw her ten minutes before the rehearsal started she was healthy as a horse\! She was even smoking—oh, her heart must’ve suffered an attack from all those cigarettes she smokes\! I’m sure of it\! I told her countless times not to smoke, and she does not listen to me. . . But she cannot be dead, can she?” I looked around desperately for someone to tell me that my best friend did not just die in front of my eyes, but nobody said anything. Suddenly I had the feeling again that this had all happened before. I had said those words on this stage, and Gary had tested her pulse. . . Was life really so cruel to rob me of Lucy in minutes, and jest at me for it? There were too many thoughts racing in my head. Too many.  
“Who doesn’t smoke cigarettes, though?” Charles finally spoke. “I, I reckon that her nerves had been exhausted from all the practicing she was doing day-and-night.”  
That was not satisfying to me in the slightest bit. “Let’s get out of here, this creepy, spooky place\! We need to call the police\!” I rose, and ran frantically to the exits. I leaned against the panic bar of the steel door. Yet, nothing happened. I tried again. The panic bar, which normally can be pushed in to open the door, was not moving at all.  
Even more thoughts, incoherent, were now flowing—flooding through my head: too many, too many thoughts at once. Thus I was especially grateful when Charles rose to the role of our emergency leader, and announced that Mary and I should check backstage for exits, while the rest of them would test each of the exits of the main auditorium.  
I walked down the hallway again, and the spookiness came again. Suddenly it dawned on me. Valiant effort at an exit. Someone among us. What does this all mean? I could not concentrate. How am I supposed to explain the fact that I’ve seen this sign already? My head was muddy, and I walked to the end of the hallway almost mechanically. As anticipated, the strange notes were still taped to those doors. I tried to open them. These don’t open either. My hands started shaking. I’m not usually someone who gets scared easily, but everything that happened today. . .  
I shook my head. I have to keep a clear head and a sharp mind. Someone among us. If this is truly a murder—it pained me just to think of that word—then I must keep a vigilant eye. Looking at the notes more closely, I tried to apply Charles’s analytic powers. The handwriting is a decently neat cursive, written with a certain style that I couldn’t quite put into words—now, wait a minute. I stepped back. This looks almost exactly like my handwriting\! My eyes widened at the realization of the similarities. What is going on??  
Suddenly I heard footsteps. It was Charles, coming down the hallway towards me. I considered briefly whether to tell him my findings, but decided against it. He would surely take it as evidence that I staged these notes—which I didn’t. The note was already on the door when I accidentally wandered here before the play. My handwriting is neat, and it’s completely possible for someone else to have the same handwriting as me.  
“Do they open?” he called.  
“No, but there is something. . . something interesting, intriguing, on these doors,” I said as calmly as possible.  
He narrowed his eyes, and in a few seconds was standing in front of me, reading the signs carefully. “Hm, what do you suppose this means?”  
“I—” It was impossible to say the word “murder” out loud, but he seemed to understand nonetheless. Nodding, he sank into deep thought, as I heard footsteps again from the rest of the people. I located Mary within the small group, and although I did not have the faintest bit of hope, I still addressed her, “Were the doors on the other side of the backstage open, Mary?”  
“No,” she answered flatly, “but there is a sign on the doors—oh, you have one here too?” She stopped in her tracks as she saw the signs on the doors, but she didn't actually look surprised. “Mine said something different. It said ‘I’m watching.’”  
Someone is watching us.  
The once familiar faces around me have turned foreign, and I tried to scan them for indicators of guilt. But I again thought of Gary. He was nervous this entire time—but he could not have done such a brutal thing to Lucy\! My blood boiled with the thought that someone could be so ruthless as to strangle such an angelic soul, and I made a vengeful oath to myself that I, Walter, will find the murderer.  
It was Julia who broke the silence that suddenly descended upon us. “Are we in danger?” she asked faintly.  
“Not under his watch\!” Anne grinned and gestured at Charles, who gave the tiniest smile before turning back to examine the notes on the door.  
But Anne’s grin really made me nauseous. How could anyone grin in such a situation—when we are in immense danger\! But I suppressed my rage and reminded myself again that the murderer is among us. I must pay extra close attention to the details.  
“The person is a rightie,” announced Charles, still analyzing the sign up close, “and the paper is fading yellow, which means. . .”  
“Psst,” Anna was whispering under her breath at me, “he’s so smart isn’t he. Do you know how he comes up with these things?” I turned to glare at her, but she was already back to gazing lovingly at Charles. For a split second, I wished my glare could tear her apart—  
Charles was looking at me silently, and for some reason I felt nervous. But he moved on, and soon I discovered that he was examining our faces, one by one. I followed his gaze attentively, trying to think like him and spot tiny pieces of incongruence in the people around me. Although I found nothing, Charles seemed satisfied with his inspection.  
“What do you make of this?” he whispered to me. “This is most perplexing indeed, and I do not like it.”  
“I think it’s very likely that Gary has something to do with this,” I whispered back, and laid out my reasons for my suspicion.  
“Very well,” he said. “I too have a hypothesis, but I still need more information.” Raising his voice to speak to the entire group of people, he ordered, “Can everyone describe their movement today?”  
 “Where is Gary?” interrupted Julia. Sure enough, Gary was nowhere to be seen. I frowned.   
“Could you go find him?” I asked.  
Julia didn’t look well, and I thought she would surely decline, but instead she answered with a firm yes, and headed down the hallway.  
Charles cleared his throat. “I’ll start. I was writing my PhD paper this morning, then I came to the theater, but by the time I got here, Lucy and Walter were already in the green room. We chatted, Gary came in, and then Mary, Anne, and Julia. Shortly before you guys went on stage, I joined Walter in the auditorium.”  
I was next. “As Charles said, I was here a few minutes earlier than him. Then I left to—” What should I have been doing then? “Then I left the green room to admire the theater. I was joined shortly by Gary, and we chatted until Charles joined us.”  
“I came into town only this morning,” said Mary flatly, “and came straight here. There was nothing in between. Anne, Julia, and I got here at the same time, and I stayed in the green room with everyone else until the start of the show.”  
“Same. Detective, why are you asking us these questions?” Anne complimented in a sweet voice.  
“It is as simple as means, motive, and opportunity,” I interrupted her impatiently. Does she ever think about what she does? “Charles is investigating who has the opportunity.”  
Charles nodded with a smile, and peered down the hallway. “Julia and Gary are still nowhere to be seen. . . Let’s go search the green room. Their statements can wait.”  
And so we all went through that wooden door again, into the green room. It was clean, with nothing out of the ordinary. But Charles was already on the move, frowning. He reached under where Lucy sat, and held up a bag I have never seen. “Whose is this?” he asked.  
There was perfect silence in the room. It belonged to none of us. Charles set down the bag and started searching through the contents. A stack of large, neatly folded T-shirts and other clothes took up most of the space, and Charles took his time inspecting each of them. There was nothing unusual. Charles looked inside the bags again and retrieved a stack of vinyl records. “Does this look familiar to anyone?” he asked, right as the door opened, and Gary came in with Julia. Gary was visibly distressed, and Julia too, but upon seeing the records, Gary completely froze in his steps.  
“Those—Those are mine\! How did you get hold of those?” he stammered.  
“We found it in this bag,” Charles answered, pointing at the bag on the floor, “which was next to Lucy’s road trunk.”  
“This must be a joke of some sort,” Gary said resolutely. “But these records do have my initials on them. . . My clothes too? Her favorite T-shirt?” He kneeled next to Charles and scratched his head.  
“Hold, there is an envelope under all the vinyl records,” declared Charles. He soon stood up with it in his hands, and read, “To Gary.”  
Gary looked even more confused at that information, but he stood up, took the envelope from Charles’s outstretched hand and read it over.  
“She was breaking up with me,” he finally announced with a neutral voice.  
What? “But she was so happy to see you earlier\!” I blurted out. “This—this all—everything is ridiculous. I have *never* met you, and now Lucy is breaking up with you?” I was again overwhelmed by thoughts, and I could feel my pulse in my head. Did he stage the breakup letter? He doesn’t even look upset\! Is this an evil prank? No, no, that cannot be\!  
“To be totally honest, this all is some sort of mistake.”  
The voice came from Gary, and I shot my head up to look at him. He had sunk into one of the chairs, but looked up at us as he said those words. “Oh, no, no, no, I don’t mean it that way,” explained him quickly, as he heard a collective gasp from us. “I mean, we met at a bar, and she just. . . fell head over heels,” he sighed. “I, too. One could say I was blinded by love, but we had no future, and I realized that only not too long ago. I know this is a terrible time, but I do miss her. I really do.” He stared blankly into the floor. Mary looked at him suspiciously, and I nodded to her as our eyes met. Although it is not impossible that Gary was pouring out his heart to complete strangers, the story is rather cliché, and he delivered it completely devoid of emotion.  
“I get it,” said Julia bluntly. Silence fell over the room again.  
If Gary were the murderer. . . “Have you seen the sign on the doors?” I asked him.  
“No, I haven’t.”  
“We should check your handwriting against it,” I suggested.  
“What is this?” There was an edge of alarm in his voice as Charles interrupted my train of thoughts, and pointed at something on the table.  
A vial.  
“Whose is this?” Charles asked, and was again met by silence. I looked around. Everyone is in the room. That must mean someone is lying.  
Charles held the vial in his hands thoughtfully. “It looks like a very well-made vial. It is newly opened, and looks like it’s bought today too based on how shiny the surface is.”  
“Is she poisoned?” asked Julia suddenly.  
Poison. Again my skin tingled with fear, and I thought I felt the weight of a pair of eyes on me. The murderer is watching. What is this vial? And suddenly it clicked. It was the perfume I bought her. It was no poison. But how am I to reveal this information while not making myself look suspicious? It’s a dilemma with no way out.  
Again it came to my attention that Charles was looking at me and seemed to be expecting a response. I silently told myself to pay attention, and asked Charles to repeat his question.  
“I said, what do you think of this new information?” he whispered, and I could not tell whether he had confirmed his hypothesis. Turning my attention to the people around me, I again sought to think like Charles.  
Julia is looking at the ground while tapping her foot. She is most definitely innocent: there is simply nothing that implicates her.  
Anne is biting her lips and staring at Charles’s shoes. Her obvious lack of respect for Lucy almost made me angry again, but I forced myself to remain calm.  
Mary is frowning at the perfume with contempt. She is also muttering something under her breath. That is suspicious, and she was jealous of Lucy for a long time, but she doesn’t have the opportunity, does she? Could she be lying about something?  
Gary is looking at the vial of perfume confused, but is the confusion feigned, or is it genuine? I could not tell, and I deemed him most suspicious. But I could feel there was a piece of the puzzle missing. What could it be?  
“I don’t have a definite idea, do you?” I replied finally.  
Charles glanced at me, and picked up the vial again. “As Sherlock Holmes said, ‘When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth.’” He glanced at Anne, who had her mouth open, but shut it out of embarrassment. “I’m afraid you don’t understand this principle, Walter.”  
However improbable. . . What could that mean? A chilly feeling suddenly ran down my spine as I remembered some realization I had when I was reading the signs on the steel doors. What was it—  
But Charles was already searching through Lucy’s handbag. “What is this?” he muttered, and pulled out a small piece of paper, torn off from the corner of a page. On it were three simple words: “Your last act”.   
A threat note?  
Again the font was nearly identical to my own handwriting. Oh right, I had thought that the handwriting on the steel door matched mine. What could that mean?  
Then the feeling again. I’ve seen those words and the paper. Where—  
And it all clicked.  
“I know where that is from\!” I yelped. “This is clearly a tear from the letter I wrote to Lucy\! I wrote something like ‘Your last act was brilliant.’ I completely forgot to give it to her, and—” Suddenly I realized how bad of a situation I put myself in by declaring that, therefore I thought it necessary to add, “I'm innocent\!” The lights in the room grew darker and I was sweating profusely. I could hear no sound other than my own pulse. I must prove my innocence. How? I turned suddenly towards the door, and stormed out of the room, towards my seat in the auditorium. The hallway seemed like a tunnel, a dark tunnel. I had never felt so lightheaded before as I fumbled through my bag for the letter I wrote at that corner store—the only item that can clear me of suspicion. Finally I found it at the bottom of the bag, and I held it up victoriously. I could barely read the words on them as I opened up the envelope with trembling fingers. Gary was right next to me with the torn off paper, waiting to see if the tear matched my letter. I ripped apart the envelope. Inside was my piece of paper. Torn.  
Suddenly the silence seemed too loud, and the world started spinning right as Gary raised his hand up to my letter, and showed that the tears matched.  
I hadn’t torn off the piece of paper. I hadn’t written the signs. I bought that vial of perfume.  
Had I?  
And the world went black.

I opened my eyes, to a uniform shade of brown. Blink. There is a figure kneeling in front of me. I seemed to be hearing something, but I wasn’t sure. Blink. There is indeed a man in front of me. Where am I?  
“You are safe. Please do not exert yourself,” said a voice. Where is the voice coming from?  
Blink. I’m in a building. There are more people standing around me.  
I’m in a theater.  
And pieces came back, how my life was left in ruins in hours, how an afternoon to catch up with Lucy left me stranded at a crime scene.  
Blink. Now why am I sitting on the ground?  
“You fainted, sir. Please remain calm,” said the same voice. I fainted. Fainted?  
“I wish to say a few words, about how I came to this conclusion,” declared a familiar voice. Charles. Right. He was leading us through the investigation.  
“I wish to first establish that Lucy was poisoned. There was simply no other way that she died at the *end* of a play.  
“Initially, I suspected Gary. He definitely had the strongest motive. But I quickly ruled him out after we found the vinyl records. His handwriting on them was not similar to the writing on the doors at all. Next I eliminated Julia. She clearly just has a soft spot for Gary’s pain. As for Anne, no offense, but she simply does not have the capability to execute something this intricate.  
“Mary, however, was my prime suspect. She has a motive: she’s clearly been jealous of Lucy for quite a long time. She certainly had the means: I could see her arranging this cat-and-mouse game with the utmost precautions. But does she have the opportunity? She drove into town this afternoon and came straight to the theater, without ever seeing Lucy. Besides, Walter and I were backstage for a significant amount of time, long enough that administering any sort of poison there would’ve been impossible under the watch of five people.  
“And with that, I have eliminated the impossible. Whatever remains must be the truth. However sorry I felt for my friend, I was then quite sure that Walter killed Lucy.  
“Walter left home this morning with a vial of poison and his letter, which he would use for proving his innocence. He arrived at the theater very early, and tore off the corner from his letter. He had time with Lucy alone backstage. As he was chatting with Lucy, he quickly sneaked the piece of paper into her bag. Lucy presumably found it later, but dismissed it as meaningless. Walter’s poison was disguised as some sort of flowery perfume, as I could tell by my sense of smell, and as Lucy excitedly put it on, the clock started ticking.  
“Then, I arrived unexpectedly, but Walter had already done the deed. I must confess I did not suspect anything. Next, it was Gary who came into the room. Again, under the watch of two people, from this point on any attempts at a murder is plain absurdity.  
“As we were talking, Walter abruptly left the room to go back into the auditorium. However, when I was leaving a minute later, I caught a glimpse of him at the *other* end of the hallway—the end that had the signs on the door. Thus he had a perfect opportunity to set up those signs as well. I went back into the room, for I thought that something was not right, and that concealment is the best strategy.  
“It was not until after a couple more minutes that Gary left. He presumably found Walter in the auditorium as expected, but it is clear that Walter had a time window to set up the signs on the *other* side of the backstage, as well as locking all the doors from the inside. Gary’s appearance also served as Walter’s alibi, if I hadn’t made the earlier discovery.  
“After the tragedy happened, Walter was first to test the doors and report they didn’t work, suggesting some prior knowledge. Then I foolishly dispatched him to test the door at this side of the backstage, so he had another time window to lock the doors, and set up the signs. But I soon realized my mistake, and came to check on him. Thus he did not have time to remove the vial from the green room, and because of this, he looked visibly agitated when I saw him.  
“Unsurprisingly, we found the vial in the green room. Again I could distinctly feel how nervous Walter is. He kept silent as we found the vial. But as I discovered the note in Lucy’s bag, he saw a chance to prove his innocence by showing that the paper was torn from his letter. But this was simply invalid in the first place, for as we saw, he could’ve torn it off himself. His nerves were so disturbed, however, that he fainted right after carrying out his plan.  
“But what is his motive? It is as simple as a matter of vanity. How would you feel when your best friend parted paths with you, pursued her dreams, and became the local star, while you are stuck at a late-hour office job? With an unclear head, it is easy for one to make such a rash decision.  
“One last thing I want to point out. Walter had made a strong impression upon me that he truly believes he did not do any of this. Therefore, I humbly suggest to you, sir, to consider evaluating Walter for the condition of Multiple Personality,” concluded Charles while looking at the stranger in front of me.  
I listened to this monologue with my jaw dropped, and I could hardly believe what I’m hearing. I opened my mouth to defend myself, but I paused to consider what Charles said. Did I truly, *unknowingly*, murder my best friend? My eyes lost focus again, as I tried to process everything that had happened.  
“Sir, sir, please relax,” said the stranger, still kneeling. “We are the police, and we were notified by the custodians. You are safe and will only be convicted based on the rules of law. But first, let’s get you outside. The evening air will calm you down.” He turned to another stranger standing behind him, who was presumably his colleague. “Please escort the rest of the people outside also. We will question them later.”  
And so I was helped out of the theater, into the night. It was pitch black, except for the dimmest moonlight that vaguely illuminated the path in front of me.

“And that is my story,” I said with a sigh. The fog in my head still has not cleared. “What if. . .” I pondered aloud, “Gary had an accomplice? —I’m truly innocent\!”  
There was a figure approaching us from the depth of the darkness. It was a boy, carrying a stack of newspapers. I looked at him confused.  
“Sir, is this the new theater?” asked the boy.  
“Yes, what do you need?” The policeman stood up and looked at the boy with his hands on his hips.  
“I’m looking for. . . Walter. A gentleman left a letter for him and told me to deliver it.”  
Me? A letter at this hour? “I’m Walter. Who is the letter from?” Could it be my boss?  
“I do not know,” the boy said as he handed me the piece of paper.

*Dear Walter,*  
*I’m terribly sorry for all that has happened today. I was full of regret as soon as she fell on stage. However much sympathy I had left for other humans, it still brought pain to me knowing that I'll have to hurt you to execute the rest of my plan. But before you criticize me, you in particular should know that no criminal will admit to their guilt: you told me none of the things you saw.*  
*But I suppose you deserve to know the truth. . .*  
*Remember when you remarked, at that restaurant a year ago, that our friendship was not the same? You were right. It was not the same, because I crossed a line and let myself fall for her. Now that I’m thinking about it, that was the start of all my troubles.*  
*She let me know right then that she would never change her mind about me. I held on to hope, like all the lovers that I had silently mocked before had. She was cunning, and took advantage of me. There was no saying no to her, and she knew that. She accepted all the presents I bought her, and asked for more and more. Money is not a concern for me, but I was keenly aware of how quickly she was ruining my life. Thus when she introduced me to Gary, with an expression on her face that said “I win,” I decided she could not win at all. If she jests at me with every day she’s alive, I shall curse her every day she’s dead.*  
*I had studied human attention in great detail, and with no intents to offend, I must say that it is rather simple to manipulate the attention of someone like you, simply because I know you so well. As preparations, I had sold the batch of perfume, postcards, and letter paper to Mr. Jones. He was the most respectable gentleman, and I regret to have him involved in such a hideous affair.*  
*This morning, I was not at home writing my PhD paper at all. I first packed a poisoned box of cigarettes for her, then I set up the flowers in front of Jones’s Goodies, brushed off the dust on the right side of the sign for the store, and arranged the items in such a way that they would catch your attention. Sure enough, as you passed by, you decided to come in, and buy the things I manipulated you to buy. You wrote the letter on the spot as I expected—you always carry around that pen—and I easily inferred what you were writing by “accidentally” passing by you a couple times. Originally I had only planned to tear off a corner, but you gave me the perfect opportunity to pass that as a threat note. You never noticed me, until you lifted your head. But you still didn’t recognize me because of my disguise.*  
*I followed you to the theater. With another trick of attention, I threw you off balance enough that you completely forgot about your letter, but your subconscious saw all the makeup on the table and prompted you to give her the perfume. After you left, she remarked to me that you referred to her cigarettes as “rich people’s cigarettes”. How ironic.*  
*A few days before, I had already broken in with keys I easily stole, and taped the signs to the doors. So all that I had to do today was to lock the doors from the inside, which I did in the few minutes I had. But you discovered those signs too early. That was not part of my plan, and I found out as I was leaving the green room and caught a glimpse of you at that end of the hallway. However, I thought quickly, and decided to use that as additional evidence against you, betting that you will not tell anyone your findings because you dread conflicts. I was right.*  
*After she was dead, I dispatched you to the hallway, so I could have time to tear off the corner of your letter. Then, I walked as quietly as I could down the hallway, and entered the green room. You were so disturbed by how similar the handwriting is to yours, perhaps, that you did not perceive me. I quickly disposed of the rest of the cigarettes, snuck the piece of paper into her bag, and joined you, before anyone could’ve had a chance to see me. And with that, the last piece of the puzzle was complete. All that is left is misdirection, and I did it flawlessly.*  
*So farewell, my friend. You will never see me again. You may submit this letter to the police as proof of your innocence, but seeing how easy I was able to slip away, they might still force you through a trial.*  
*P.S. Do you really suppose I did not understand social interactions to such an extent that I could not pick up on Anne’s crush on me? With all due respect, you never knew me like she did, Walter.*  
*Forever indebted to you,*  
*Charles*

If someone told me the story of my life could take a greater twist after Lucy’s death, I wouldn’t have believed them. Yet here I am, sitting on the concrete road, reading a letter that just made the worst day worse. Slowly, I put the letter down, and laid back to rest my head on the cold concrete. The clouds had cleared, and the night was starry. For a moment I forgot about the rest of the world.
