# To Whom It May Concern | September 16 2026

You sat on the edge of your seat, head buried, scribbling away. Faces that you never cared about chattered harshly at the table over, and you wished the world could turn it down by a few decibels. Nibbling at the end of your pen, you scoured for ideas you deemed important enough to record down on paper, to be read four years later by yourself.  
I want you to think about what you wrote, and how much of it still matters to you.  
Those things didn’t last forever, did they?  
Life is long when you are seventeen. After all, four years account for a fifth of your life. But it won’t always be this way. Some day, as you read these words, your hands will tremble, and your hair will not be hanging over your forehead and tickling your eyes. It’s inevitable. So is some twenty years of laughter before that happens, and maybe twenty more. You don’t have to worry.  
But although you’ll get old, words won’t age. So I want to remind you of the things that I hope also won’t age for you.  
August 9th, let’s start there.  
It was a long drive to Pasadena. For more than six hours, the engine rumbled over the soothing music, and you could barely hear when *Eleven Eleven* comes on. You always wondered how loud the engine must be, if normal volume on the I-5 can throw kicks and punches at your eardrum once the street finally starts having traffic lights.  
You planned your entertainment well. You will read the book you pirated onto your phone, and then you will ride out the good energy by giving that personal statement some edits no one will ever notice. You were anxious. Really, really anxious about the uncertainty that loomed over you. So you flipped open the book, and decided to let it be a distraction.  
It was an ordinary British boarding school tale, taking place some time in the past. Ellwood and Gaunt, trying to figure life out. It was going to be relaxing.  
Except it was 1914, and WWI was about to start. You watched in horror as 18-year-old Gaunt turned himself into a soldier, and every letter of that word was revolting. You gawked at the blood and bodies. So did Gaunt. But “\[a\]gainst the vast spread of history, Gaunt’s fears dwindled into insignificance.” Terrified, you sighed a mixture of horror and awe.  
I hope you remember that “marshmallow punch to the chest” you felt as you shut the book on the last page, Pasadena streets rolling into view. Remember how you weren’t anxious anymore. It’s just college. Nobody will die.  
But I hope your life isn’t just about the vastness of the universe and your unimportance. I hope it’s also about you.  
August 21st. It was the tail end of a Bay Area summer, which meant a chilly breeze at half past six. The sun would set over distant mountains in a few hours, but for now, windows and blurry patches of goldenness seeped through the tree branches and took footrest on your shoulders. There was exactly one old lady playing catch with her dog in the park. A soccer lesson was wrapping up, and elementary schoolers were scurrying around, finding entertainment in the mundane job of cleanup. They had a huge smile on their faces.  
You had foregone your usual outfit of a hoodie, and instead wandered through fresh grass and bird songs in a T-shirt. With no deadlines and no hurry, you threw your frisbee around aimlessly, singing to yourself. The distinct lack of echo made it much more enjoyable. Your eyes traced the path of a honeybee, taking flight after stopping at a wildflower. The air simply smelled *good*.  
Eventually, at some point, you were alone. The entire park, just you. And the world seemed so big then. The clouds shone pink, and a low-flying plane waded through them, its engine sounded more comforting than usual. You started to sing again, this time at the clouds. You didn’t have any particular song in mind; you were just trying to express the clouds and trees and fresh air through fourths and eighths. You had a huge smile on your face, too.  
I hope you remember that serene smell of wet grass and how it felt to breathe the evening sky. I hope you remember the feeling of dissolving into music. I hope, wherever you go, you remember how to sigh contentment.  
To the 17-year-old you, that is enough.
