# Strangers | October 21 2026
San Francisco nights are perfect. It wasn’t and never would be too cold for a stroll from the Giants stadium back to the Caltrain station, and I absent-mindedly sauntered through the October streets in perfectly comfortable silence with my friend Luis, while the tunes of “Take Me Out to the Ball Game” still rang in my ears.
It was way past ten o’clock when, pinching my nose to the acrid smell of marijuana, I finally greeted the San Francisco Caltrain station. The number of people boarding the last train had at first surprised me, but after a while you get used to the incessant stream of people flowing into the ticket gates. Under the dimly lit station platform, this stream would soon disperse and go back to a thousand different lives across the Bay Area. I’m just one of them.
It was not long after Luis and I started examining Jung Hoo Lee’s poor performance against Shane Drohan, I think, that I received a light tap on the shoulder.
The stranger behind me was half a head taller than me, and the somber, yellowish lamp above him cast deep shadows on everything except for his glasses, which had a golden frame. He was virtually shuddering in a thin coat, but as I turned around, he smiled a warm smile that quickly faded into the wind. He seemed to be expecting something, something I distinctly failed to give him as I stared at him in a mixture of confusion and annoyance.
“Hey,” was his first word, after a silence so long that it felt like the world had stopped, waiting for him to speak. Seeing no reaction from me, he tried smiling again, but it didn’t reach his eyes, “I’m Henri. I thought you’d remember me. Ah, we were friends at Blossom Hill Elementary?”
The disappointment in his voice was quite irritating, but the mention of my elementary school’s name gave me pause. Henri? Henri Poisson? The boy that was always talking to me about niche facts about different kinds of airplanes and annoying me out of my skin with them? I inspected the man in front of me. Needless to say, everything about him had changed, maybe except for those glasses he treasured, still with golden frames. I have more than a bit of trouble reconciling this person, looming over me, with the childish Henri that lives in my head.
I must have made a face, because he grinned, and in that broad grin I saw traces of the boy, who always beamed so brightly when he talked about those useless plane facts.
“I—” I paused. “Good to see you again\! I, I do remember you, just—you look really different.”
He smiled fondly, “You too. Forgive me for eavesdropping, but you—I recognized you by your voice, the thing you do when you get analytical about baseball. And, yeah, sorry.”
I had forgotten Luis was standing awkwardly by my side the whole time, but at this point he took a step forward and extended a hand, a stupid smirk on his face. “Luis. We were just watching the Giants Brewers game. Were you there too?” I made a why-are-you-grinning-so-much eye contact with him, which only made him smirk harder.
“Ah, no. As a matter of fact, actually—I actually have to fly out tonight. My aunt passed on Monday, and she named me in her will… so I must settle some paperwork in Dijon. Oh, uh, French countryside. I just saw my sister off.” He smiled sadly but made himself meet my eyes again. “There’s a very high chance that I’ll move to France, but I was here for the past few years. What about you? I didn’t know you were in the area.”
“My condolences… But wait, you said you live in SF?” I gestured wildly to the city outside this station, incredulous. “I thought… I thought you went to France after Blossom Hill\! Did you not?”
“That I did. But a few years ago, I was given an opportunity to work here, so I moved back here immediately. California still has a particular attraction to me. Especially here, I guess. San Francisco nights are perfect.”
“I know right\! The only thing that could top them, I guess, are those evenings where I played soccer at Rinconada with all of my friends until the sun went down. All we could see was blurry blobs moving around in the darkness, but what fun we had\!”
“That was quite fun. I missed playing soccer with you.”
A pause.
I started uncertainly, “So. Um, we’ve got to catch this train, but do you want to exchange numbers? It was fun talking to you, maybe we could catch up sometime?”
“Oh,” Henri’s eyes widened for a brief second, then he almost sighed and laughed at the same time. “I mean, I don’t think that would be of any use. I simply wasn’t quite part of your life in Blossom Hill, I suppose. You lost my phone number; you didn’t even remember that I was one of the people who played with you at Rinconada. But that’s all right, it’s been some two decades. I understand.”
He took a deep breath.
“Because you, perhaps, will never see me again, please allow me to tell you the truth. For all five years of Blossom Hill, I had a crush on you, like a foolish, *childish* boy. I knew you thought I was a tagalong for bugging you about airplanes so much, and I know, even at this moment, you think that I was quite a bit silly. But truly, all I wanted was your attention, even for one brief second. Alas, it seems I never got it. You were never going to remember me.
“But as I said, I understand. When I tapped on your shoulder just now, I saw the image of a *stranger* through your eyes. I shouldn’t have expected anything different. But I still do. I still do.”
Henri inhaled sharply through his nose again, and for a moment, he looked off into the darkness, where the train was headed. His eyes glistened briefly in the light.
“Well, I’m terribly sorry to make your evening so sour, but fate crossed our paths, and now they are about to diverge forever. So I simply wanted you to understand. How did I expect you to react to this information? I do not know; I’m still quite foolish. Perhaps, I would rather you just walk away and board your train, without a single word. Whatever you do, though, I understand. It’s perfectly reasonable that you forgot about me. It would be better if we were strangers.”
It wasn’t until Henri had fallen into a deep silence that I realized that Luis had poked me when Henri was speaking. “TRAIN,” he mouthed at me, and tilted his head towards the icy shell of the portal, ready to take me back to Blossom Hill, back to the life I’ve always lived. Through the window, I saw an older man with a beard, a woman following him down the aisle, wearing heavy makeup and sunglasses on her head. To their right, you could see faint reflections of Henri and the station lamp. Henri had his head downcast, long shadows drawn across his face by the lamp. His coat seemed thinner than ever as he suppressed his shivers as hard as he could, but for how tall he was, he appeared fragile.
Almost—heartbreaking.
Henri met my eyes when I turned back to look at him, but I didn’t have long before I heard a familiar sound. It was beeping, and it was from the train doors. Train. I abruptly broke eye contact, and clumsily dragged a stumbling Luis into the warmth of the train. The door closed behind us, and I felt some vague sense of pain from the cutting of a tie that never existed. White light oozed out overhead and made my world brighter, but I caught sight of Henri, still with a sad smile, still in the dim, yellowish shadow, still with those golden framed glasses. For a moment I wanted to yell through the window that I still remember him, behind the playground slide, telling me that the A380 has two floors.
But sensing the crowd of passengers standing behind me, I ultimately didn’t. As the city of San Francisco started retreating, Henri looked up and waved a goodbye, his hand frozen in midair. I opened my mouth and raised my hand to wave back, but he had already looked away, swallowed by the darkness of cold San Francisco nights.
