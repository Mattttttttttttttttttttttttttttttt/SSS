# **Untitled | May 27 2026**

He sits in his chair, propped his head on his hand. Looking out, the wind is swooshing by in the backyard. It’s a beautiful evening, but he’s not going out. He never goes out with his parents. Walks are boring. He never liked walks. Aside from when his cousin came over.  
His head is a bit heavy. From all the work, maybe. But there isn’t even that much to do. He finds himself in this strange situation often. Forever worrying but never doing.  
He sighs and stands up. It might be time to get coffee.  
“Ouch\!” He looks around. Blink. It’s pitch black. What?  
Suddenly he’s aware that a thick liquid is surrounding his hands. He’s inside his bedroom wall.  
Tentatively, he takes a step into the darkness. There is no echo. No sound, even. It feels incredibly strange to him. It’s like how he felt on that roller coaster: how scary it was when gravity is taken away.  
Deeper into the darkness. He finds his coffee, on a white, round table that is just there. He didn’t notice that when he came in. Sounds are emerging. He hears the sound of the wind, and the sound of people screaming. He turns around to a giant roller coaster, and his cousin in front of him, trying to play catch with him.  
The air is so sticky and thick. He wants to move his hand around, to catch the frisbee that’s coming at him now. It’s flying so slow. He blinks. So slow. He reaches out, but his hand is in a gel. The frisbee is slicing through the gel like an electrical drill, and the sparks of light it emits are so bright that it’s hurting his eyes to look at the frisbee. He turns away, looking for coffee.  
He realizes he never took the coffee. It is still on that white, round table. But it’s bigger now. In fact, it’s almost as tall—and if you look at it from another angle, it’s even taller than him. He has to climb to the top to get the coffee. He sighs, and grabs onto the lower part of the mug handle. Pulling himself up, he stands five feet off the ground, and begins to climb along the handle. It’s very slippery and cold, though. Cold and almost like water. He looks up, he has climbed so high that there is snow on the mug, And the mug itself has frozen over. Coffee\! Won’t it freeze also? His arms are hurting.  
Red lightnings zap through the sky. Suddenly it is clear to him that what he thought was the darkness was actually muscle fibers, and the lightning is more intense now. It’s started to rain. The rain droplets are so miniscule that he cannot even catch it. He knows they are coffee, though. Sticking his tongue out, he cannot taste anything, but it is wet. And cold.  
He redirects his attention back to the snowy hiking trail in front of him. He is getting to the summit of this snowy mountain. He takes a step, but the snow is too deep. Suddenly he’s sinking through the snow, struggling to no avail. The snow around him collapses into a tunnel, guiding him down to a mineshaft. It is pitch black again, and he cannot feel the walls.  
Suddenly he jolts awake. He sighs, and stands up to get some coffee.

# **The Foam | June 4 2026**

The air was cold, but Rosalind’s palms were sticky with sweat. Piles and piles of paper, mountains, even, surrounded her. The messy state of the laboratory would shock any outsider, but Rosalind got used to it a long time ago. At least she keeps her test tubes clean. Between her and herself, she would say she’s a pretty organized person, but she also admits that the situation doesn’t look good for her. In her defense, she couldn’t go outside during the day. Just look outside the window. Snow this year was unusual even to her town, and it had collected to several feet thick. Just as well—she didn’t have anything special to do, and she was secretly thankful that the snow gave her such an excuse to not go to the telephone booth to call home.  
Spring was nearing, though, and Rosalind hoped that her work could be drawn to a close soon enough that she could go home for the first time since ten years ago during the summer. Not like she had any particular ties with her family, but it’s clearly in some sense inappropriate for her to go ten years without seeing them. Tonight. Tonight will be the night, though.  
Her hands were shaking so much that she had to set the test tube back on the rack. It’s just routine cleaning, but with these hands she will be able to get nothing done. She pulled over a chair and sat, closing her eyes. Deep breaths, Ben used to always tell her when she gets nervous like this. She sighed. Why did she have to think of the painful memories right now? She was about to see him again. Deep breaths, Rosalind.  
But her mind could not let go—it never did, and perhaps never will. She remembers every detail about the way he looked in the chair in Brooklyn. How he smiled, and how they laughed about the adventure he’s about to go through. How, after he crossed, he stood up and ran in horror. How she rushed up and watched in disbelief as his beautiful body fell in her arms. And that was it.  
She always knew he had heart problems, but it didn’t become real until the moment she was told that he died of his heart simply *stopping*. God, how she would’ve done differently\! She had since grown certain that Ben was the love of her life. What a beautiful life they would’ve had, had his love not turned destructive. She can’t believe that it ended like that. An oversight. Oversight\! That word will haunt her until the end of her life. Tonight, tonight, prayed Rosalind, may the mountains of papers prove to be my protector, my guiding spirit. May I succeed in achieving what has never been done. May I open up the door to anything and everything.  
She looked down at her hands. They are steady enough to do the final setup. She felt strangely confident, despite the wholly unpredictable nature of her undertaking. Deep breaths, she reminded herself. Never get ahead of yourself.  
She had labeled the wires carefully for the past week, but she needed to do one final check that they are all correct and that no one had tampered with them. Not like anyone could have. There was quite literally no one else in this building—everyone had gone home, except for Chris on the second floor. But he wouldn’t be here until six in the morning. Even during the summer, aside from Chris, no one would stay up this late. It’s an hour (maybe two?) until sunrise. By the time the sun rose, she would have revolutionized everything.  
“Ingenious.” Ben had said. As she looked at the ugly box in front of her that can do magic, she increasingly felt like Ben was right. Bulky and unwieldy, this was nevertheless the gem that she built herself. A quantum chamber with Planck scale cavities is connected to a quantum program. It’s essentially a simulator. That part was easy, though. She thought as she connected the circuits. The box buzzed, and the crude status indicator flashed yellow.  
She walked over to the other end of the room, where another identical box resided. As the status of the second box also turned yellow, she suddenly felt a sense of surrealness. This is really happening. She kneeled down to open up the small fridge that the box was placed on. Opening the ice maker, she took out a sealed test tube with some fluid in it. That was the hard part to design. A fluid she can drink, that delivers to her brain the robot that will assemble the electrodes in there. The contents are smaller than a human cell, in order to fit through her blood vessels. She reminisced about the many nights she spent, scratching ideas and redesigning. When the eureka moment hit, she was in disbelief. She could not believe that she, Rosalind, out of everyone, would be the first one to bring simulation to reality. Oh, no. Much more. Targeted simulation. No one else will be able to see, hear, or feel any of what she will perceive. It’s a simulation device that happens in her brain. A constructed reality, just for her.  
She often imagined announcing her findings to the public. Every word and phrase she would use to impress upon them that this is the groundbreaking scientific breakthrough that she has created. Many are bound to be disappointed. To them, simulation has to be real. Every time she thought of the idea, she scoffed silently. “Real”\! Another play on words. Those people are standing on clouds as if it’s solid ground. Do they really think that a mere reference to “reality” can dismiss what she has created? When you then ask them to define what they mean by “real”, they will dodge the question or mumble something insensible.  
Enough about other people, she reminded herself. This is my moment. She unplugged the test tube, and drank the contents in one gulp. Pulling the chair over, she sat and stared at the yellow status light. Any moment now, it will turn green, and the boxes’ quantum field will have successfully entangled with the electrodes, and she should be in blissful heaven.  
But until then, it’s an excruciating wait.  
It takes roughly a minute for the fluid to be absorbed, despite her best attempts at reducing that time. She’s not a biologist, after all. Then, it takes a little time for the components to all gather in her brain, which should make reality feel a little different momentarily, and after a few minutes, the fog will have cleared, and the simulation would start.  
She looked around the room. There is a clock on the wall opposite to her. It looks quite vintage, but she doesn’t remember it being there. The pile of paper also seems to be tilting at a weird angle. She stared at it, and it seemed to always be toppling over, but when she blinked, it always stayed upright. Intriguing. Suddenly she remembered how Ben and her bought that vintage clock on the wall at München. It was an afternoon—no, it might have been at a night market instead.  
She snapped awake as she saw that the pile of paper was in fact tipping over, and was traveling fast towards her. She raised her arm to catch the paper, but she couldn’t move. Indeed, she couldn’t even turn her head to dodge. Well, the pile does seem to be moving slower now, and now they have stopped falling entirely. It looks like they have actually fallen upwards, because the pile is definitely tilting less now.  
“Hey, starlight.”  
Rosalind stared at Ben, in front of her.  
She did it. She crossed. For a moment she was speechless. The sky had turned a blueish gray hue, and it lit up the side of Ben’s face. That silhouette woke up something deep in her subconscious; she had missed this, missed it every day for ten years.  
She stood up from the chair and ran into his arms. How nice does he smell—he’s still wearing the same sweatshirt he wore that day. It’s like a reunion with an old friend—literally, actually. All the different puzzle pieces, all the different memories she had of him, broken, are now one whole being, standing in front of her, in her arms. She felt tears come, and she sniffled. When she cried, he would always say—  
“Let it out, Rose,” said Ben. “You did it. You made it.”  
Through teary eyes, she looked up at him. His face is in the shadow, but she could trace every contour with her eyes. “Yes,” she said through a sniffle, “I made it.”  
Ben smiled. “Does that mean I’m living in your simulation? It sure doesn’t seem different from the real life.”  
It’s exactly what he would’ve said. But again, her thoughts drifted to the public. “It’s all just a simulation\! Nothing’s real\! People in your simulation aren’t alive\!” Yet, as she felt the fabric on the back of Ben’s sweatshirt, she decided—no, she *knows* this is real. This is reality. And her invention will have an amazing impact on the world. It will be the best thing the world has had since the invention of TVs. She will make it portable, and commercialize it, and then she will never have to—  
She snapped out of the daydream from agonizing pain. It felt as if a thorn was pierced through both her hands, and she could barely keep her eyes open, but what’s clear is, her hands are intact. Nothing’s wrong with them.  
“Dear Rosalind. We meet again,” enunciated a deep voice.  
That’s not Ben. Rosalind forced her eyes open to find both the pain and the thorn gone. In front of her was an—existence. It was not like anything Rosalind had ever. . . perceived. . . before. It was entirely something new, she was sure of it. She felt the presence, in some way, but if she really strained her eye to look, there was nothing. At the same time, she knew exactly where the existence was, what it was doing. The *voice* that she perceives was much similar: it seemed to bypass her sensory system entirely, and it was an absolutely mind-bending sensation.  
“I feel like I should introduce myself,” the existence declared as Rosalind felt it shift into a more humanoid form. It was tall enough that Rosalind had to look up at it. Which is weird, because the ceiling is not particularly high in her lab. But a look around soon revealed the cause: she had become shorter. “I am the Patroller of Space Foam, as the English translation goes. (Your language is much funnier than you realize, Rosalind.) Your idea, the very idea that you could mass produce your invention, is dangerous. You will succeed. For a few short years the human civilization will have reached its peak. But then Maxime will be born. And what he will do to the machine will trigger such a deformation in the Foam, that our entire civilization—if you could call it that—will be at risk.  
“Fortunately, this is quite easy to prevent. Thus am I here. But first, before I begin speaking, I wish to persuade you against any possibility of *escape*. This is my world, Rosalind. I could make the clock you looked at disappear. I could make the pile of paper fall to the ceiling. I could make the ceiling disappear entirely and we could watch the sunrise together. I am, however, benign. As long as you are compliant.”  
Rosalind paid attention to the existence in sheer confusion. All the floors above her were gone; she was now staring at the night sky, moments before sunrise. The night wind brushed against her cheeks, and she felt the surrealness again. It feels too real, yet all of this is simply *impossible*. . .  
“If that feels real, I could also make you experience many sensations at once,” the existence announced as Rosalind felt the adrenaline from free fall and the g-forces from a rocket launch simultaneously. Gravity is absent, yet it is also present and much stronger than usual. And just like how fast it happened, now the sensation is gone.  
“Again, I must persuade you to consider the facts.”  
Rosalind perceived the sounds of the existence absent-mindedly. She had heard these arguments before. Where—  
“That’s a dangerous train of thought, my dear.”  
Ah, she knows. It’s from the villain of one of the movies that she watched with Ben.  
“Rosalind, you are proving to be more frustrating than I anticipated. Humans are such a hassle compared to everything else,” the existence radiated impatience. “The choice is simple. Keep the inventions secret and live a long, beautiful life with your Ben, or decide to pursue your ambitions and die, here, in this building. Consider your *genius*. You will be able to change the world in a different way, if you simply decided to keep the simulator secret.”  
Die? Here?  
“Yes, die.”  
How?  
“Rosalind, have I not impressed upon you that this world is mine? I’m literally reading your mind by simulating into the future—something you decided wasn’t possible (and something you will not achieve). Please consider the sensibility of my argument. It’s death or a picture perfect life. Why can you not see the appeal?”  
You have a point, Rosalind thought. I will keep it a secret. Before she even finished the thought, she turned over and pressed down the on/off switch of her simulator. The simulation might be yours, but I shall return to reality. She smiled to herself.  
Her fingers touched the cold night air. She turned, to see that the machine was not there at all. Neither was the fridge. Neither, for that matter, are the piles of paper, or the test tubes, or the racks they were standing on. The room looked like when she first moved in. Emptiness. Except for that chair she sat on when she drank the fluid, to convince her that she’s not dreaming.  
The sky was brighter now. If this was a fever dream, she should be awake soon.  
“My dear Rosalind,” she felt the existence sigh, “Why must we go through this? The simulation is deterministic. *You have no escape.* But how simple it is. Accept my proposal. Keep the secret to yourself. Live your life. What more need I say? Do you wanna end up just like Ben?”  
Just like Ben?  
“I was not so tolerant of your work ten years ago. The mere tampering of the Foam was unacceptable, and he was promptly killed to give you a warning. It appears, however, that you did not take the warning. So here we are today. I gave you the reunion earlier. And I decided, you can have it for a lifetime if you want to—Ben growing old with you. You just have to do me one favor. Why are you so stubborn?”  
Because that’s your life\! It would be your life that I’m living, not mine\! I have free will, and I will use it, or die. Ben died for a cause, and I will not let him die in vain\! Rosalind tried vainly again to feel for the simulator box behind her, but it was simply not there.  
“My dear Rosalind, I wish we have longer, for maybe then I can change your mind. But it appears we don’t have long. Chris will come into your room in around 30 seconds. So the question is: exit the simulation to go back to your dream life, or die here? Hurry up and make the right choice. You do not want to die a gut-wretching death in front of Chris, do you?”  
In front of Rosalind flashed Chris’s face. He is only 22; there is time for him. Rosalind wanted to think. But her hands picked up the chair behind her, and threw it, with all her might, against the existence.   
The chair disappeared midair.  
“Very well then,” she felt the existence telepathize from behind her, “it appears that the human’s notoriety for their insistence on ideals and the abstract is well-deserved.”

It was barely sunrise when Chris walked into the lab building. He felt the need today to get up a bit earlier, to challenge himself with a changeup in his routines. And it worked. As he cleared the snow in front of the building’s winter entrance and let himself in with his ID, he felt much better, with a much clearer mind than usual. In fact, he decided that he will do this every day from now on. It would be great if he could have such a conscious mind every morning. The cold really wakes him up.  
As he passed by Rosalind’s lab room, he even felt cheerful enough to say good morning to her. People have told him that she was beautiful when she was younger, but an accident a decade ago had decimated her life, and now she was living a life of suffering for the singular hope that she might discover something groundbreaking. In a sense, he felt sort of similar to her. They both have great ambitions, and he absolutely admires the drive she has about her work, and the hours she put in. If anyone asked him, he would admit without hesitation that she was a much greater scientist than himself.  
Which is why it was such a shock to him as he stepped into her lab room to a gaunt Rosalind standing in front of a bulky box. Wires were everywhere. This place was the messiest lab he had ever seen.  
He was still frozen silently at the entrance, processing the events, when Rosalind suddenly picked up a chair from behind her, and threw it as hard as she could across the room.  
It hit another similar box at the other end of the room. The box crumbled. A huge boom was heard by Chris before Rosalind and him were engulfed in flames and debris.

# **The Game | June 7 2026**

I’m Sahil, and I’ve seen it all.  
You are reading this on paper—I found a notebook at the doorstep of the apartment. Yet anyone around can hear me. I’ve learned to turn off my thoughts so I do not get discovered, but I’m not as good as some. They made thoughts louder than a whisper, and the more adrenaline you have in your system, the louder. It has led to some gruesome incidents that I witnessed.  
But who cares? If someone were to find this notebook—I’ll be leaving it here after this next Update—I can only tell you that you do not know how lucky you are. Or will They find this as well? It’s always a Patrol. But after all, They have to keep the show running and the audience entertained. At our expense. Expense. What a great word. We are expendable to Them.  
As I’m writing this I can hear my thoughts getting louder again. I must remember not to get riled up. Patrols will hear me and They will have a good laugh about it. Or someone will find me.  
That’s the thing. The human population is dwindling—They have the genome of a normal human but They won’t release it until the population gets down to maybe 1000 or so? Instead They clone Hunters into Earth. And what Hunters they are. I cannot count the amount of times that I’ve seen a Hunt. It’s the most gruesome thing ever.  
I’m getting ahead of myself and too loud. I must stay low.  
Let me tell you something more tranquil: the Rules of the Game.  
It’s fairly simple. The world is Updated every 12 hours, and during those 12 hours, there are no Updates. That means I cannot see anyone else move at all. I live in slices of time—if you can even call this situation living. The world is a 3D image that only updates every once in a while.  
And now the catch. When the world updates, if you are overlapping with anything in space, you die instantly due to memory overflow at the overlapping grid points. Now if an object has been moved by two different people, it returns to its original position. Those are the rough rules, of course there are technicalities, but that’s how a Hunt works. The Hunters are remarkably good at being quiet. Maybe They cloned them to be quiet, but I’m pretty sure I had heard a Hunter scream before. Maybe they were a Rogue. Either way, it doesn't matter. They will kick down walls, move physical things that you would never think to move right where you are crouching, and even cramp themselves into the same space as you, just to bring you out. I’ve seen plenty, dear Lord.  
But I must stay quiet as I continue to tell you these things. They rearranged the continents to create the Map. All the developed countries and a few others are squished together into one big, rectangular Map. All I do every Update is run around this place and avoid Hunters and other people. Sometimes I team up with someone else that I come across—you can always tell humans apart because we are so loud. But naturally, forming a team is both difficult and dangerous in our circumstances.  
They picked the countries for a reason. There are so many structures, so many places to be. When I was still young, I was through two weeks in a row with someone—dear Matthew, may he rest in Heaven. I remember him clearly. It was him who taught me how to whisper in thoughts. We climbed up a crane for one Update, and I walked to the shorter end while he walked to the other end without realizing. It all just sounds the same up there in the open. And after the Update, I looked around for him earnestly, but I saw no one. No one. Gone, just like that. You just can’t hear the Hunters coming. I screamed his name that night. It broke my heart.  
That was a long time ago, however. Back then you could still just, run into people. Now that’s once in a blue moon. I don’t know how many people are left, but last time I heard someone new, he was up in the Eiffel Tower, probably climbed up there the day before. He was praying before an Update, I think. His thoughts were whispers, but, granted, the world is completely still. And so the Hunters got him right there right then. It’s just like that.  
I don’t remember where I came from or who taught me the Rules. I don’t remember anything, really, except for all the things I’ve seen that would’ve broken young Sahil’s heart. But I’ve seen it all. I’ve let myself fall off a skyscraper for a full story, just to avoid a Hunter. The air is still and the world is too, so it wasn’t all that dramatic. But as I looked up after the Update, I saw her falling. Three feet from the building. She couldn’t have touched the building if she reached out, and indeed she was never trying to. She was just trying to reach me. To make contact. And how close she was. She’s been falling from the roof, and she was only a foot or two away from me. That was one of the closest I’ve gotten to dying. And I could bet that it made it onto the Broadcast.  
I’m sure They drop supplies into places from time to time, because when I smashed the windows with my shoes and slid inside on sharp glass, I found a first aid kit in the cabinet, even though clearly, the office had been rummaged at some point before. I spent an hour or so cleaning up, and I set out on my way. I don’t know how many Hunters there are, but judging by how often I encounter one, there are quite a lot. They vastly outnumber the humans, I believe. But their abilities vary from Hunter to Hunter, and some are loud enough for me to pick it up. The adrenaline is really a hassle, though. I’ve learned to suppress it to a certain degree, but, you know how it is under adrenaline.  
That was also one of the first things I learned—from Matthew. Now that I think about it, I haven’t had a proper friend in a long long time. But that makes sense. They always seek to keep you on edge. Always something chasing you. They love to see your expression, that first face you make when you hear thoughts that aren’t yours in the same room as you, a minute before an Update. I’ve come to believe that the Hunters are programmed to do that intentionally so that the Broadcast is more entertaining. Really. They never produce a sound until the very last minute.  
Speaking of a friend, I definitely have not had any since 42 Updates ago. I am fully loyal to Them and will unconditionally obey Their commands, even when it comes to the *existence* of our species. I have not found anyone else since, and I also do not split up with anyone before Updates to avoid Hunters. There are no reunions needed after Updates. I have never talked, with anyone or to myself, anything expressing disloyalness to Them. I have never intentionally avoided Patrols because they do the Broadcasting, either. And I’m sure that with Their Highness’s high emotional intelligence and situational awareness, They can correctly interpret my message here. I sincerely hope that NO human finds this notebook one day after it’s all over, because I do not want this to be over and I’m enjoying the Game very much.

Dear friend, we have time for only a couple more anecdotes before the Update happens, so I shall be brief and quiet. All of the things Hunters do that I said in the beginning, I’ve seen them all. There are Golden Keys scattered throughout the Map, and when you obtain one, you gain 14 Updates of real-time world updates. That means you can spot Hunters and outmaneuver them more easily. But I’ve seen this other boy with a Golden Key. I’ve tried to teach him how to whisper, but he just couldn’t get it. He had a Golden Key then, but he got his earlier than mine, so I had 2 Updates of watching him, and warning him of Hunters. But it happened anyway. A Hunter chased us into an area of broken down walls. I was quiet, so really the Hunter was onto the boy. At the last second before the Update, the boy leapt over a wall, and I thought he was safe. But then, the Hunter simply kicked the wall open. It was just a wooden box, really; it was hollow on the inside. And one of the pieces overlapped the boy. Gone.  
Or the last time I saw Lucy. I met Lucy in Egypt, and we stayed together for quite some time until about 1,000 Updates ago. We were somewhere near Rome when she, with a Golden Key we found in Athens, spotted a Hunter, clearly onto us. The area was suburban, and I don’t think there was anywhere to run. The Hunters really appear out of nowhere, you know. The Hunter was on our heels, and it was a few minutes until the Update, so she told me to leave her. I ran inside a house—all the doors are just unlocked for entertainment’s sake—and locked her and the Hunter outside. Through the bug-proof open window, I heard her thoughts get fainter and fainter, until eventually they vanished into the distance. Perhaps she turned a corner. Whether she survived, I don’t know. I’ve never seen her since.  
Dear friend, this may be the Update when I have to say goodbye—I always tell myself that. It is in less than a minute now. If I survive, I’ll talk

# **Once upon a Time, There Was a Pond | June 28 2026**

i.e., *id est* introverted extrovert.  
Now, many people seem to be irked by this mish-mash of contradictions. Since I very, very rarely encounter anyone with the same energy as me, allow me to define this term for you, by describing myself.  
Here’s a thought experiment I had. Suppose everyone was unwilling to talk to strangers. Then, of course, no friendship will be formed. It would, indeed, be extraordinarily nice for interesting people to just strike up a conversation with you. But in order to appear interesting to interesting people, you have to not blend into the crowd. So although I’m only too good at turning invisible, it follows from logic that if I were to make any friends, I have to be the person that goes to talk to people. I have to be the one to find out who I like enough to be my friend.  
I’ve been trying for a few years now to form a somewhat scientific theory of making friends. On a conceptual level, I don’t understand how people formed such close friendships I see around me. Well, I’m aware that this is no way to think about friendship. Science is too cold; friendship is warm. But I still want to know. And my dad provided an excellent insight from a book he was reading: small talk. You have to do small talk to be able to craft a bond strong enough to pull the person close for a deep conversation. But honestly, I hate small talk. More specifically, I dislike the kind of small talk where you talk about weather, sports, movies. First of all, who is really that into weather? It’s just a game that both sides agreed to play. Second of all, you have to be decently invested in one team to have a meaningful conversation about a sport, whereas I’m just the type of person who understands the rules of a lot of sports, and watches the highlights. Sports is a pastime, when I don’t have anything better to do, watching sports sounds nice. But I’m at a place right now where I always have something better to do. Lastly, to quote what I said a billion times, “I hate movies”. More on that later.  
Another insight came from my best friend. He said, “Trust doesn’t grow looking around for it sadly. It does by sharing and showing care.” I think that is wise. Showing genuinely care sounds much better than playing the game of small talk. Besides, if I want to look for people who care, I have to first care for them.  
I also have a love-hate relationship with the idea of competition. Most of the time, I prefer to not think about it as a competition at all. I’m there to have fun, to do my best, anything but to *compete*. “Competing” sounds like conflict. I don’t like conflicts. Well, I don’t like conflicts when they are unprompted, as is the case with competitions. Besides, isn’t this a much healthier mindset? My livelihood doesn’t depend on me being world class; I can just do whatever.  
More generally, this extends to adrenaline. Now, I do like adrenaline, but only in the form of nerves encountered when I have to do something that’s challenging, be it public speaking, performance, or competing. I quite like those feelings. It’s someone you run to for heartbreaks and setbacks, someone you can talk with about things like the theory of friendship. Someone you can be unapologetically yourself with. And there’s the in-between friend. Maybe you go grocery shopping with them, and you update them on something you told them before. It’s someone you’d invite to a housewarming party, and hug when you see each other.  
In some twisted, idealistic way, I refuse to have an in-between friend. Not the least because, I dislike a big party where I don’t know most of the people well. If just smiling is enough to maintain a friendship, then I’d be so well off. But I have to put in my limited energy—I only have so many spoons.

# **Villa de Vecchi | October 7 2026**

The sky was barely visible through the canopy as Heather trudged slowly through the tall undergrowth. On her eighteenth birthday, her cousin Amy had told her about the house—Villa de Vecchi. She whispered in a hushed voice that if Heather ever went there, she would have a permanent curse put on her. It was made clear that it was not a good thing to do, yet it was delivered with a spark in Amy’s eyes. So naturally, Heather disregarded the warning, but she never had a chance to actually take the trip, as she still had to complete her coming-of-age ritual in her tribe. To call it a house was for sure a misnomer. Due to the dense forest, it was not until she was at its foot that she could see the house in full. Mansion, that’s a better word. Its walls shot up to heights of three or four stories, white paint peeling off everywhere. There was no doubt it was abandoned a long, long time ago, maybe even before she was born, although not likely: she knows what a decade of moisture could do to a mansion like this, let alone when the mansion was right on the coast of a medium-sized lake.  
The sun was still up in the air; it was maybe three o’clock. She had gotten quite good at telling the time recently, thanks to the teachings of her mother, Maret. The sun was right in the middle of what Maret referred to as “the limbo”: not right on top of the world, but not setting any time soon either. Hence, at the latest it was half past three, which means she had at least an hour to herself, inside this mansion.  
She took a deep breath, and did a brief spell and prayer to clear herself of bad energy. Then, she pushed open the front gate.  
A deep groan from the door latch greeted her, almost in a dead man’s voice. Half expectant of something dramatic to happen, she instead just felt a disconcerting energy. By the light through the gate, only a couple couches and tea tables were visible, scattered around. Dust balls sat in the middle of paths, and as the door creaked closed behind her, the image of them rolling away reached her by the last sliver of light. Suddenly finding her in the darkness, she swallowed hard. The only source of light was the daylight creeping in from around the dark curtains obscuring windows. Despite her fear, she repeated again and again Maret’s words: darkness is the residence of ghosts.  
She listened.  
And sure enough, within seconds, the darkness responded, with a violent racket of things crashing down one after another at some distance. Heather winced. Did it come from upstairs? She hesitated, her eyes narrowing, trying to make out silhouettes and outlines. But a tentative step was taken towards the window, and she pulled the curtains aside, determined.  
Now that she had a fuller view of the mansion, its past glory was obvious. A rosy red carpet now almost faded gray extending forwards into the depth of the house. A large circular table was to her left, an empty vase in its center. Next to the door on her right was two rows of hangers, an abandoned hat hanging from the top row. Where the carpet led, she could see couches, armchairs, and more tables.

Leaning against the arm of the armchair across from her, there was a pile of magazines, the top one flipped open, corner curling up, edges wrinkly from the moisture. A radio, its layer of dust visible on top, stood haphazardly in the center of the chairs on a short table.  
A flash.

She snapped back.  
Granted, this was not the first time she’d received an image from the underworld. Yet this one felt… different, somehow. It was a lot of strong ideas rather than vivid imagery, fuzzy shapes and outlines instead of vibrant people and objects. She felt uneasy, but she pressed her lips together and told herself to straighten up. “Pilgrims only set out to the worthy,” She muttered Maret’s words.  
There was another faint noise, this time definitely from upstairs. Heather even thought she heard muffled curses. A shiver ran down her spine. She repeated her good omen spell a couple times, and decided to head upstairs.  
The stairs were trying—the railing was too dusty to hold, and some staircases were even tilting down due to their age. Someone sprayed writing on the wall, and it read, “A. WAS HERE”. The darkness of the ink formed a striking contrast with the fainting yellow of the walls, as if it was the void.  
Fortunately, the second floor was almost as spacious as the first, because there’s no way she could stand long, narrow hallways right now. There was less furniture here, but she saw marks on the ground, suggesting the presence of chairs in the past. A flash.

A flash.  
Warmth and comfort. The hallway, sideways. Waning crescent outside.  
Heather brushed her hair back, and headed into the hallway, shaking her head.

Its doors were not shut, but the content was mostly cleared out, aside from an opaque cylindrical box and a stack of note paper. The room was dreary despite a small version of a chandelier hanging from the ceiling. A third of the glass on it was missing. She felt repulsed by the room, so she practiced good omen again, and left.  
The second and third room, to her disappointment, were each a barren, dusty bed and nothing else. There was nothing on the walls, and they appeared to be better preserved than the main room.  
As she was about to walk into the fourth room, however, it happened again.  
A flash.

Unmistakably, there were faded red blotches all over the floor by the wall to her right. The peeling paint was even dyed a dark crimson. It felt like someone splashed red paint all over the floor. It felt like someone moved the boxes out of the way. It was blood.  
A flash.  
Darkness—maybe a blindfold. Screaming and shouting, coming from way up in the attic. She felt her heart pounding. Someone was cooing, but every scream pierced her heart. She felt the flow of pain from whoever was screaming. She felt stabs at her heart instead of her mother’s.  
Her mother’s? But—

Behind, Villa de Vecchi loomed over her, demanding an answer. Heather demanded an answer too. But to whom? It was to be understood that no one could offer her the answer she was looking for. In the end, she had resolved not to mention to Maret a word about the mansion. Maybe if she forgets, she can forget the curse the mansion put on her, too.
