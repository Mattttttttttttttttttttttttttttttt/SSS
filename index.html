Villa de Vecchi

1,913 words · 9 min read

The sky was barely visible through the canopy as Heather trudged slowly through the tall undergrowth. On her eighteenth birthday, her cousin Amy had told her about the house—Villa de Vecchi. She whispered in a hushed voice that if Heather ever went there, she would have a permanent curse put on her. It was made clear that it was not a good thing to do, yet it was delivered with a spark in Amy’s eyes. So naturally, Heather disregarded the warning, but she never had a chance to actually take the trip, as she still had to complete her coming-of-age ritual in her tribe. But that was finished a few days ago, so on this day when her mother was out for some errand, perhaps, she set out in the direction Amy pointed at.
To call it a house was for sure a misnomer. Due to the dense forest, it was not until she was at its foot that she could see the house in full. Mansion, that’s a better word. Its walls shot up to heights of three or four stories, white paint peeling off everywhere. There was no doubt it was abandoned a long, long time ago, maybe even before she was born, although not likely: she knows what a decade of moisture could do to a mansion like this, let alone when the mansion was right on the coast of a medium-sized lake.
The sun was still up in the air; it was maybe three o’clock. She had gotten quite good at telling the time recently, thanks to the teachings of her mother, Maret. The sun was right in the middle of what Maret referred to as “the limbo”: not right on top of the world, but not setting any time soon either. Hence, at the latest it was half past three, which means she had at least an hour to herself, inside this mansion.
She took a deep breath, and did a brief spell and prayer to clear herself of bad energy. Then, she pushed open the front gate.
A deep groan from the door latch greeted her, almost in a dead man’s voice. Half expectant of something dramatic to happen, she instead just felt a disconcerting energy. By the light through the gate, only a couple couches and tea tables were visible, scattered around. Dust balls sat in the middle of paths, and as the door creaked closed behind her, the image of them rolling away reached her by the last sliver of light. Suddenly finding her in the darkness, she swallowed hard. The only source of light was the daylight creeping in from around the dark curtains obscuring windows. Despite her fear, she repeated again and again Maret’s words: darkness is the residence of ghosts.
She listened.
And sure enough, within seconds, the darkness responded, with a violent racket of things crashing down one after another at some distance. Heather winced. Did it come from upstairs? She hesitated, her eyes narrowing, trying to make out silhouettes and outlines. But a tentative step was taken towards the window, and she pulled the curtains aside, determined.
Now that she had a fuller view of the mansion, its past glory was obvious. A rosy red carpet now almost faded gray extending forwards into the depth of the house. A large circular table was to her left, an empty vase in its center. Next to the door on her right was two rows of hangers, an abandoned hat hanging from the top row. Where the carpet led, she could see couches, armchairs, and more tables.
She ventured towards what looked like the dining area, but instead, she found herself sitting down on one of the chairs, her gaze wandering around the room. This was clearly the living room. Leaning against the arm of the armchair across from her, there was a pile of magazines, the top one flipped open, corner curling up, edges wrinkly from the moisture. A radio, its layer of dust visible on top, stood haphazardly in the center of the chairs on a short table.
A flash.
The radio playing Rete Rossa. Another woman behind her was singing a popular song that the radio was playing. There was laughter around the short table. The room was well lit and glamorous, armchairs looking brand new.
She snapped back.
Granted, this was not the first time she’d received an image from the underworld. Yet this one felt… different, somehow. It was a lot of strong ideas rather than vivid imagery, fuzzy shapes and outlines instead of vibrant people and objects. She felt uneasy, but she pressed her lips together and told herself to straighten up. “Pilgrims only set out to the worthy,” She muttered Maret’s words.
There was another faint noise, this time definitely from upstairs. Heather even thought she heard muffled curses. A shiver ran down her spine. She repeated her good omen spell a couple times, and decided to head upstairs.
The stairs were trying—the railing was too dusty to hold, and some staircases were even tilting down due to their age. Someone sprayed writing on the wall, and it read, “A. WAS HERE”. The darkness of the ink formed a striking contrast with the fainting yellow of the walls, as if it was the void.
Fortunately, the second floor was almost as spacious as the first, because there’s no way she could stand long, narrow hallways right now. There was less furniture here, but she saw marks on the ground, suggesting the presence of chairs in the past. One corner of the huge room was raised, and on the platform, what remained of a piano lay upside down, barely recognizable. Indeed, if she hadn’t walked up to it and taken a closer look out of curiosity, she would’ve mistaken it for an anonymous pile of wood.
A flash.
Piano, playing a song—maybe Beethoven’s Tempest Sonata? More laughter. She knew the sound was familiar, but she couldn’t quite grasp exactly what it sounded like. It was a concept, floating into her head from the emptiness of the mansion. She felt that the image felt nice, pleasant, content, which was the first time that the underworld sent her emotions.
She put it aside, not knowing what to make of it.
This floor, she noted, had a wide hallway to the right. It was open. She glimpsed a bed in one of the rooms that lined the hallway. Must be all the bedrooms.
A flash.
Warmth and comfort. The hallway, sideways. Waning crescent outside.
Heather brushed her hair back, and headed into the hallway, shaking her head.
The first room honestly had ample space for another grand piano. A wooden shelf stood by the far side of the wall, fading in color. Its doors were not shut, but the content was mostly cleared out, aside from an opaque cylindrical box and a stack of note paper. The room was dreary despite a small version of a chandelier hanging from the ceiling. A third of the glass on it was missing. She felt repulsed by the room, so she practiced good omen again, and left.
The second and third room, to her disappointment, were each a barren, dusty bed and nothing else. There was nothing on the walls, and they appeared to be better preserved than the main room.
As she was about to walk into the fourth room, however, it happened again.
A flash.
And actually, multiple of them. She saw the room through what felt like a dream. The room was fully furnished, and the two single-hung windows opposite to the door let in the beautiful lake scenery, onto a softness. She looked down, and indeed there was a carpet. The room was telling something to her, but her witchcraft was nowhere near advanced enough to pick it up; all she could feel was that she was hyperaware of the room. She could imagine, wordlessly, how it felt like to sit on that bed by the corner. She knew how this room felt like when it was furnished to the inhabitant’s preferences. She could see herself, sitting in front of the bed, the desk, and lying on the carpet where the windows cast their light. It felt like a lazy afternoon.
She paused in confusion. The room looked nothing like what she “saw” it to be. A big ugly piece of the ceiling paint lay squarely in the center of the carpet. The curtains to the windows were half-drawn, but she felt like she could see through them. The bed lacked the mosquito net and the light purple bed sheet that she saw so vividly; rather, it was completely bare: nothing except for the wooden framework and headboard remained, not even the mattress. The bedside table was there, but she had felt the presence of some toy on it that she did not see. Worst, the desk was utterly nonexistent, yet squares of depression remained on the carpet, signaling its ghostly presence.
She felt shivers creep up her spine again, so she practiced the good omen a few more times before she quietly exited the room, not daring to go into it any further.
The third floor likely served as a mere attic, for there was significantly less space compared to the previous two floors. The floor was all but empty, save for a couple boxes scattered here and there. Tall and well designed windows reached from floor to ceiling on the lake side, and judging by the sun, it was about to be half past three or so. She was so worried about returning on time, but it seemed like the concern was misplaced: she still had half an hour before she needed to head back. Taking a deep breath, she smiled.
Which is when she saw it.
Unmistakably, there were faded red blotches all over the floor by the wall to her right. The peeling paint was even dyed a dark crimson. It felt like someone splashed red paint all over the floor. It felt like someone moved the boxes out of the way. It was blood.
A flash.
Darkness—maybe a blindfold. Screaming and shouting, coming from way up in the attic. She felt her heart pounding. Someone was cooing, but every scream pierced her heart. She felt the flow of pain from whoever was screaming. She felt stabs at her heart instead of her mother’s.
Her mother’s? But—
Good omen. She tried to clear her head and do a spell to calm herself, but before she could do so, in a split second, as if a dam was broken, emotions and images came roaring into her mind, crashing against whatever structure she had built up. It was contentment and coziness mingled with fear, anger, and helplessness. It was people she had never seen, yet they felt like they should be her family. It was a blurred awareness of some irreversibility. It was chaos; it was unfamiliar intruders, mushed by her own tears.
It was knowing: “Gone are the days.”
The sun was approaching four o’clock when Heather finished her meditation by the lake. The water was tranquil today, the wind was gentle. All the overgrown grass here by the shore of the lake was familiar in a distant sense, even though it was across from where her tribe is.
Behind, Villa de Vecchi loomed over her, demanding an answer. Heather demanded an answer too. But to whom? It was to be understood that no one could offer her the answer she was looking for. In the end, she had resolved not to mention to Maret a word about the mansion. Maybe if she forgets, she can forget the curse the mansion put on her, too.
